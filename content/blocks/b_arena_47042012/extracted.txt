Executive Summary
The neurotechnology field’s immediate focus must remain on developing clinical therapies to restore
function and alleviate suffering. However, achieving the technology’s full potential to improve lives
globally requires a deliberate path from this essential clinical foundation into a broader consumer market.
To successfully bridge this chasm, this roadmap outlines how consumer access can only be unlocked
through the non-negotiable guardrails of clinical utility, rigorous and transparent regulatory approval, and
uncompromising patient trust.
The 5-year vision is to establish a viable consumer neurotechnology ecosystem grounded in clinical
validation. This requires developing credible "dual-benefit" technologies that serve both therapeutic patient
needs and future consumer demand. To fund this transition, early-stage consumer demand will act as a
financial bridge, where a small cohort of privately funded users de-risks new applications and provides
the vital capital necessary to make clinical platforms widely accessible. Responsibly governed neural data
can strengthen this bridge by enabling multiple high-value applications, from targeted neuromodulation
and improved diagnostics to surgical planning, assistive interfaces, and AI. These broader partnerships can
attract new R&D resources, accelerate clinical maturation, and expand the path to responsible consumer
impact.

1 Beyond Treatment and Toward Human Flourishing
The mission of clinical neurotechnology companies is noble: to develop therapies that restore movement,
communication, and brain health. Deep brain stimulators (DBS), for example, have become an accepted
therapy for disorders like Parkinson’s, with over 200,000 implants worldwide alleviating symptoms for
patients.[1, 2] This clinical work is the foundation on which the future of the field will be built. Yet, while
clinical healing is the necessary first horizon, to confine the ambition of neurotechnology to the clinic would
leave its greatest potential for human flourishing untapped. The ultimate promise of neurotechnology lies
not only in healing the sick but also in empowering the healthy.
One of the most compelling cases for consumer neurotechnology lies in providing precise, effective, and
safe solutions to deeply human needs that are currently met by crude, systemic, and side-effect-laden tools:
chemicals. Humans already spend hundreds of billions of dollars annually seeking to modulate their own
brain states. Examples include:
• Precision Relaxation and Focus:Consumers use chemical stimulants and relaxants to manage their
daily lives. The U.S. caffeinated beverage market, a tool for focus, was valued at approximately $80B in
2025.[3] The U.S. alcoholic beverage market, a tool for relaxation, exceeded $350B in 2025.[4] These are
systemic interventions, prone to abuse, with myriad undesirable side effects.
• Precision Sleep:The desire for "sleep on demand" is a massive, unsolved problem. Consumers spend
vast sums on chemical sleep aids, which act systemically and can create long-term dependency. The
global sleep aids market is valued at >$80B for 2025.[5]
Neurotechnology creates the opportunity to mimic the desired effects of these and other chemical
interventions (focus, calm, restorative sleep, etc.) with precision, on-demand, and without the risks of
systemic chemical tools. The stakes, therefore, are not about creating new consumer desires. They’re about
capturing a significant portion of the half-trillion-dollar-plus annual market that consumers already spend
on imprecise tools for brain state modulation.
From this foundation, more ambitious applications may eventually become possible, including closed-
loop tools that support learning, richer affective computing, and higher-bandwidth interaction with consumer
technologies such as phones, computers, and electronic assistants. These possibilities remain longer-horizon
applications, outside the core five-year roadmap presented here, and should not move toward consumer
deployment until they are supported by strong evidence, robust consent, user control, and clear governance.
Nevertheless, responsibly extending neurotechnology from clinical restoration toward broader human
flourishing represents the field’s greatest economic and societal opportunity.

2 Today’s Landscape: Clinical Proving Ground and Consumer Horizon
To build a viable path to a consumer market, the neurotechnology field must first undertake a sober assess-
ment of the current landscape. Today’s environment is defined by two approaches: non-invasive consumer
devices are achieving market penetration but lack capability, while higher-capability invasive devices are
confined to clinical trials due to their risk profile.
On the non-invasive front, the consumer transition is already beginning. Meta’s "Neural Band," for
example, is bundled with its Ray-Ban smart glasses and uses electromyography (EMG) from the wrist to
provide subtle, low-bandwidth control for augmented reality interfaces.[6] Similarly, electroencephalography
(EEG) systems have long been used for non-invasive brain-computer interfaces (BCI), most notably in
P300 "speller" applications that allow users to communicate by selecting on-screen targets.[7] While these
applications can feel magical to the user, non-invasive devices have yet to deliver the life-changing capabilities
that will one day define the consumer market.
Simultaneously, high-bandwidth invasive BCIs are demonstrating transformative user interactions and
improvements in mental health and performance, but remain confined to clinical settings. Clinical trials have
successfully used high-channel-count implants to restore communication and speech in patients with severe
paralysis, by reading and decoding neural signals in real-time with high accuracy.[8, 9] Likewise, implantable
devices are being used in clinical trials to modulate brain states to treat mood disorders.[10, 11, 12] While
it’s not a massive conceptual leap from clinical brain state modulation to on-demand consumer brain state
modulation (as contemplated in the previous section), there is no established regulatory path linking one to
the other..
Box 1: The Hearing Aid Precedent
The challenge of transitioning a regulated medical device to a consumer category is not without precedent.
The most important regulatory and market playbook for the neurotech field is the recent history of the
hearing aid. For decades, hearing aids were Class I and II medical devices, available only through licensed
professionals. On August 17, 2022, the U.S. FDA issued a final rule that created an entirely new regulatory
category for over-the-counter (OTC) hearing aids.[13] This rule, which took effect in October 2022, was
designed to increase access and affordability for adults with perceived mild-to-moderate hearing loss. This
regulatory shift did two crucial things: it "unbundled" the device from the professional service, and it allowed
for consumer-led sales in retail stores and online. This coincided with a technological and social convergence:
mass-market consumer electronics, led by Apple’s AirPods, integrated hearing-assistive functionality (like
Conversation Boost and Active Noise Cancellation) and, critically, normalized the sight of in-ear compute
devices in public. This trend culminated in 2024, when the FDA authorized Apple’s AirPods Pro as the first
software-based OTC hearing aid, perfectly illustrating the med-tech-to-consumer-electronics pipeline.[14]
This convergence of med-tech, consumer tech, and social change is the blueprint. It demonstrates that the
FDA is willing to create novel regulatory frameworks to safely expand consumer access. For neurotechnology,
this precedent provides a clear objective for policymakers: not deregulation, but rather the creation of a
new, appropriate regulatory category for consumer neurotechnology, grounded in this successful model.
The challenge will be defining regulations that ensure safe implantation procedures, without impeding the
consumer neurotechnology ecosystem from scaling to support millions of users.

Encouragingly, there are signs of an accelerating convergence of clinical neurotech and consumer tech. A
2025 demonstration showed a paralyzed person using a Synchron implant with NVIDIA AI hardware and an
Apple Vision Pro to control home devices by thought.[15] Overall, the trend toward less invasive devices like
intravascular stents is critical for clinical access but may not currently be ready to deliver the data bandwidth
required for transformative consumer applications. The ultimate consumer goal is a minimally-invasive,
high-capacity interface with ear-piercing, tattoo, or, at worst, LASIK levels of risk.
Consumer adoption will still depend on whether interface installation and removal can be made safe,
simple, and reversible enough to support responsible scale. The near-term priority is therefore to generate
transparent evidence that procedures can mature into low-burden clinical workflows. Public reporting
of installation and removal room time, cost, serious adverse events, and removal outcomes would give
regulators, clinicians, partners, and future users a concrete basis for assessing risk and progress.
3 5-Year Vision: A Viable Consumer Neurotechnology Ecosystem
The principal five-year goal of this roadmap is not mass-market adoption, which will take longer to achieve.
The goal is to create greater access to neurotechnology by the creation of a viable, functioning consumer
neurotechnology ecosystem. Success in 2030 is defined by the existence of the critical components, proof
points, and pathways that make a mass-market future possible. This ideal state will be characterized by the
following measurable milestones:
• Clinical and Platform Validation.As a non-negotiable foundation, the field will have achieved at
least two FDA approvals for high-bandwidth neurotechnology platforms. A critical mass of at least
10,000 patients will have been implanted, establishing a strong safety baseline and providing the initial
platform for exploring dual-use applications.
• The "Roadster" Moment.To actively subsidize the manufacturing cost curve and accelerate innovation
for future clinical patients, the first viable, self-funded research models will have emerged. Drawing in-
spiration from the "Tesla Roadster" strategy, a cohort of at least 50 individuals will have paid significant
sums out-of-pocket for an FDA-approved device, utilized within privately funded, investigator-initiated
expanded-indication trials to document quality-of-life improvements. To ensure equitable access to
medical care, this high-end consumer model must be strictly applied to non-therapeutic, exploratory
quality-of-life and performance studies (e.g. precision focus or high-bandwidth consumer tech interac-
tivity), rather than core medical interventions intended for severe neurological deficits. The purpose of
this model is not to create a privileged shortcut around clinical access, but to test whether self-funded,
tightly governed cohorts can expand capacity, generate public evidence, and produce a measurable
patient-access dividend.
• Demonstrated "Dual Benefit" Wedges.The bridge between clinical and consumer applications will no
longer be hypothetical. At least 2-3 credible patient and consumer “dual benefit” wedges will have been
demonstrated and publicly disseminated. Crucially, to maintain public trust and ethical integrity, these
demonstrations must establish the clinical therapeutic value as the primary, undeniable achievement.
The consumer application must be positioned as a secondary, downstream extension of that success -
unlocked only after core clinical safety is established - ensuring patients are the primary beneficiaries of
the technology, not merely a testing ground for consumer features. For example, a speech BCI clinically

validated for locked-in syndrome might also be demonstrated as a platform for high-speed, silent
interaction with an electronic assistant.
• Mainstream Tech Integration & High-Value Data Applications.At least two strategic partnerships
with major technology companies, healthcare organizations, or research consortia will be active. These

interfaces, adaptive consumer software, and AI models of human cognition. By broadening the set of
clinically and commercially valuable uses for neural data, the field can attract new R&D resources, scale

• A Clear Consumer Opportunity Map.The field will have a living ”Neurotech Gap Map” that identifies
the consumer applications with the strongest unmet need, market potential, and technical requirements.
This map will help founders, investors, researchers, and funders prioritize dual-benefit R&D rather
than scatter effort across speculative use cases.
• Mature, Rapid Workflows.The procedures surrounding interface installation will be mature. Installa-
tion and, critically, removal will be routine, low-burden, reversible workflows. Clinical centers will
have established rapid protocols, with procedure room times of less than 60 minutes for installation
and less than 30 minutes for removal.
Box 2: Patient Access and Non-Displacement Guardrails
Patients are the primary constituency for this roadmap. The ethical case for consumer expansion is that it can
make BCI cheaper, more accessible, faster to deploy, and less invasive by creating commercial incentives to
improve manufacturing scale, procedural workflows, reversibility, software tooling, and long-term safety
evidence. This case holds only if consumer demand expands clinical access rather than competing with
it. Accordingly, "Roadster"-style cohorts should be governed by a non-displacement principle: no non-
therapeutic or augmentation study should consume devices, clinical slots, surgical/interventional capacity,
company support, or trial infrastructure needed by clinically indicated patients. Where capacity is scarce,
clinical patients receive priority and non-therapeutic enrollment pauses or is capped. Privately funded
cohorts should be permitted only when they create incremental capacity, such as dedicated manufacturing
runs, new center training, workflow standardization, removal-protocol development, or patient-access
funds. Programs should publicly report clinical and non-therapeutic implant volumes, wait times, removal
outcomes, serious adverse events, and the share of consumer-derived funding reinvested into patient access.

4 Roadmap: Building A Bridge to the Consumer Market
Achieving the 2030 ideal state requires a focused, actionable plan that builds consumer-market option value
without distracting companies from clinical milestones. Because no single company can fully validate the
consumer frontier alone, the following initiatives emphasize high-leverage evidence generation: validated
dual-benefit use cases, academic-industry exploration pathways, reversible workflow maturation, and a
clearer map of the consumer opportunity space. Each initiative should be structured to add evidence, funding,
or market clarity around the clinical critical path rather than impose new platform-building obligations on
companies.

5 Risks and Mitigations
A strategic plan of this scope must anticipate and proactively mitigate potential risks to its execution and to
the field at large.
Risk: Patient Crowd-Out Mitigation
Early consumer demand could compete
with patients for scarce devices, clinical
slots, technical support, or procedural ca-
pacity, worsening access for the popula-
tions this roadmap is intended to help.
The roadmap should adopt a non-displacement principle.
Consumer or augmentation cohorts should proceed only
when they create incremental capacity, and should pause
or be capped whenever clinically indicated patients face
scarcity.
Risk: Mission Drift Mitigation
High-margin consumer opportunities
could pull companies, investors, and part-
ners away from the clinical mission before
therapeutic platforms are mature, weak-
ening the legitimacy of the field.
Consumer programs should be tied to patient-relevant mile-
stones, such as improved safety, reversibility, reliability, cost
reduction, reimbursement evidence, or expanded clinical-
center capacity. Patient-access metrics should be tracked
alongside consumer-market metrics.
Risk: Two-Tier Access Mitigation
The field could create a two-tier system
in which wealthy early adopters gain ac-
cess to advanced neural interfaces while
patients face long waits, limited coverage,
or restricted clinical availability.
Roadster-style programs should include an access dividend.
A portion of self-funded consumer revenue or study fund-
ing should support patient access through subsidized im-
plants, clinical infrastructure, reimbursement evidence, or
manufacturing scale-up.
Risk: Weak Patient Governance Mitigation
The roadmap could lose ethical credibil-
ity if patients, caregivers, disability ad-
vocates, and BCI ethics experts are not
visibly involved in shaping the clinical-to-
consumer transition.
Patient and disability-community input should be built into
study design, consent materials, access policies, adverse-
event reporting, and public communications, especially for
dual-benefit demonstrations and Roadster-style cohorts.

Risk: Data Exploitation & Privacy Loss Mitigation
The lucrative potential of monetizing
brain data for AI training could lead to
predatory data-harvesting practices, com-
promising user privacy and eroding the
rigorous patient trust required for the
field to advance.
The roadmap must champion strict data governance frame-
works from day one. Monetization strategies must rely on
anonymized, aggregate, or user-consented data markets
where early adopters retain ownership and control over
their neural data. Clear firewalls must be established to
ensure clinical therapeutic data is never commercialized
without explicit, opt-in consent separate from clinical care.
Risk: Public Backlash from Safety or
Performance Failures
Mitigation
A single high-profile safety failure in a
healthy consumer, or a product that mas-
sively under-delivers on its transforma-
tive promise, could trigger media amplifi-
cation and restrictive regulation that sets
the field back a decade.
This must be countered with a phased, patient-grounded
strategy and radical transparency. Early consumer models
must be limited, clinically governed, and strictly evidence-
generating. Simultaneously, the field must treat trans-
parency as a core product: publicly reporting all metrics,
including adverse events and successful removals, to build
trust through verifiable data rather than promises.
Risk: Ethical Backlash Mitigation
There is a reputational risk that the public
will view early clinical trials as exploita-
tive stepping stones used solely to de-
velop non-therapeutic consumer applica-
tions for wealthy early adopters.
The narrative and business models must explicitly frame
consumer applications as the engine that drives down costs
and accelerates innovation for clinical populations. The
"Roadster" strategy must be positioned transparently as a
mechanism to fund further clinical R&D and improve access
for patients, rather than abandoning the clinical mission for
consumer profits.
