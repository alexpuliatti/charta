Our brains are trained to passively filter out advertising.
 (Billboard? Ignore. YouTube ad? Dissociate.) Culture hacking is the antidote 
to the unwelcomed banner ad—it’s advertising that doesn’t feel like advertising. 
I wrote this guide to put words to what cult brands do so naturally as well as to give 
insight to brands that strive to build a cult-like following. Whether you’re RIMOWA 
or Delta Airlines, this white paper articulates a way of seeing that is relevant to you.
Note: It’s important to clarify the term culture hacking. Far from illicit connotations, 
culture hacking signifies the ability to decipher cultural dynamics and craft brand 
marketing that strikes a chord with people. While many brands react to cultural 
currents, this white paper emphasizes how cult brands not only engage with culture 
but actively shape it, setting new standards for creativity and innovation.

Cult Brands Aren’t A New Phenomenon
Before the advent of social media, brands like Harley Davidson, Supreme, WWE, 
Vespa, and Trader Joe’s cultivated fervent followings akin to devotion among their 
customers. The Macworld Expo annually drew thousands eager to witness Apple’s 
latest innovations while Mazda Miata-themed weddings became (and remain) 
a celebrated phenomenon. These brands initially occupied niche markets but, 
propelled by the fervent advocacy of their followers, evolved into mass phenomena 
with dedicated and passionate consumer bases.
There’s No Cheat Code For Cultural Relevance
Over time, cult brands build strong associations intricately tied to a subculture 
or lifestyle movement. When you hear Supreme, you think of skater culture. Vivienne 
Westwood is British punk culture. IKEA embodies Swedish living and hygge. Liquid 
Death? Going out while sober. Cultivating these cultural associations takes time but 
these distinct associations become the unique selling proposition that defines a 
cult brand and creates mental availability. Cult brands excel at inhabiting a cultural 
niche and fully embodying it tooth and nail.

Cult Brands Have Convinction
In addition to selling culture, cult brands establish kinship through a tone of voice 
that embodies that of a friend; a creative atmosphere that evokes a lifestyle image; 
and cultural references that convey a distinct worldview. Above all else, across all 
aspects of brand expression, cult brands communicate a singular point of view. 
This unwavering authenticity mirrors the mind behind the brand, drawing in devotees 
who crave a clear, human perspective.

Cult Brands Aren’t For Everyone.
Cult brands have a laser-sharp focus on their most devoted customer: 
those who authentically embody the lifestyle they promote. Seth Godin refers 
to this as the smallest viable market: “the people who want your offering... 
[are] most open to hearing your message... who will tell the right other people.”(1) 
This focused approach cultivates diehard fans and money-can’t-buy devotion. 
On the other hand, brands that try to appeal to the masses make constant 
compromises and ultimately become too generalized and boring. 
Being niche kicks other competitors off the ledge.

Pressure Makes A Diamond Cult Brand.
Today there are exceedingly more brands, product categories, platforms, 
and celebrities. A casual Google search of ‘women’s black leggings’ yields 
a staggering 432 million results. Yet in this sea of limitless choice, the cult brand 
has not died. Amidst this abundance, cult brands not only persist but have 
multiplied exponentially, accelerated by the increasing speed of trends 
and the ubiquity of going viral.
Enter brands like JACQUEMUS, Aimé Leon Dore, Arc’teryx, Erewhon, and Stanley
(ugh)—a sample of today’s cult brands that epitomize the times. Are they succeeding 
solely due to superior products or is their popularity due to a blend of impeccable 
timing and savvy branding? Although product quality, branding finesse, and cultural 
timing are critical ingredients, the success of these contemporary cult brands 
reveals a new paradigm in brand marketing: a framework that challenges traditional 
strategies and redefines the path to cultural relevance and market share.

Creative Rule #1: Ideate With Empathy.
Empathize with your desired audience. What are their current needs, challenges, 
and frustrations with the world? What needs aren’t being met by the dominant 
culture? Create a list, then ask: How could your brand solve one of these problems? 
When the brand experience is built from a place of empathy, customers begin 
to trust and rely on the brand, fostering fandom. In the ‘90s, Starbucks helped 
mainstream the concept of the third space - places to gather that weren’t the office 
or home. Cycling brand Rapha connects urban cyclists through its weekly ride 
meetups. Empathy not only builds brand equity but creates a competitive edge 
in your category and culture at large.
Case Study:  Going Beyond Skin-Deep
Every year, Aesop quietly transforms a select number of stores into ephemeral 
queer libraries through its Queer Library initiative. All shelf space is dedicated 
to books written by LGBTQIA+ authors - many whose books have faced bans 
or challenges across the country. Customers browse the curated breadth of titles 
and choose one book to take home for free. (People love free things, btw.) 
I remember visiting my first Queer Library at Aesop’s West Village location in June, 
2021. The experience stood out to me as it was the first time that I was in a space 
surrounded by other queer individuals discussing gay literature. While June has 
become the marketing Olympics of rainbow washing, Aesop’s determination 
to create these community moments—in a society that may not otherwise offer 
them—gives its fans a genuine reason to believe.

Creative Rule #2: Define An Ownable Culture.
A loyalty program is not a brand culture. Rather, a true brand culture is a set of dis -
tinct rituals, community rules, and shared language that all reinforce a brand’s val -
ues and collective identity. In a CrossFit class, athletes wholeheartedly cheer each 
other on regardless of fitness level, an unspoken rule rooted in camaraderie and 
non-judgement. At my buzzy hot yoga studio, every sweat-drenched class concludes 
with a cold, lavender-infused hand towel—a subtle gesture that reflects and differen -
tiates the studio’s ethos of rewarding hard work.
The unique practices of a brand culture become second nature to its dedicated 
followers who model and socialize the culture to newcomers. One’s devotion is 
therefore expressed and reinforced through the adherence to said brand culture. 
Ultimately, an ownable brand culture—and the ability to live and breathe it alongside 
others—creates the drawcard of belonging that so many customers long for.

Creative Rule #3: Parasocial > Transaction.
Parasocial relationships—one-sided relationships between fans and a persona 
(influencers, podcasters, OnlyFan creators, brands)—are a new norm of our modern 
society. While we might see our closest friends weekly at most, parasocial relation -
ships can be a daily experience, becoming a constant source of comfort, knowledge, 
entertainment, and motivation. 
Imagine if your brand were a real person in your customer’s life. Who would they be? 
What would this person’s interactions look and feel like? Beauty disrupter Glossier 
cultivated a devout following by treating its customers as genuine co-creators—invit -
ing them into Slack channels for product feedback and making UGC a cornerstone 
of their content strategy. Duolingo’s little green owl has become the Internet’s fa -
vorite TikTok troll for his sassy and oftentimes unhinged clapbacks in the comments 
section. Cult brands behave more like humans than companies, providing a distinct 
emotional benefit to their audience.
Case Study: K-Pop’s Truman Show Strategy
If K-pop were an onion, its outer layer would glisten with its perfected production 
and immersive visuals. But as the layers are peeled back, you’d be surprised to 
discover something more raw: a constant stream of unfiltered, relatable content. 
Picture handheld vlogs, cozy livesteams in sweatpants, and casual mukbangs. 
For fans, it all feels like being part of an endless slumber party.
K-pop’s parasocial content strategy orchestrates a profound bond between artists 
and fans through regular, intimate access. BTS, the world’s biggest music group 
in history, produced a weekly show titled Run BTS that amassed over 150 episodes 
across three seasons. Each episode featured band members taking on challenges 
and secret missions, providing fans an unscripted glimpse into their lives. 
Korean music labels have mastered this content strategy, shaping the fan narrative 
through consistent emotional access to their favorite artists.

Creative Rule #4: Conversations > Campaigns.
Cult brands don’t just make campaigns; they create conversations. In a world where 
everything is viral, cult brands leverage a strategic mix of cultural timing, consistent 
output, and the draw of nostalgia to spark broader cultural dialogue.
Calvin Klein’s Instagram campaign with Jeremy Allen White, which generated $12.7 
million in media exposure in 48 hours, debuted just days before his Golden Globes 
victory.2 In 2017, Calvin released a campaign with the cast of Moonlight the morning 
after their Oscar win for Best Picture. The fashion brand’s impeccable timing enables 
media to amplify a single moment with real-time culture.
The DTC shapewear brand SKIMS generates more buzz with its star-studded social 
ads than Vogue covers.3 The brand operates with cultural agility and production nim -
bleness, quickly and frequently photographing current musicians, athletes, and influ-
encers—all while maintaining creative cohesion. For SKIMS, more is more.
Prada stoked nostalgic fervor by reuniting Scarlett Johansson with director Jonathan 
Glazer in its recent Galleria Bag campaign.The duo famously collaborated on the cult 
sci-fi film Under The Skin in 2013. Prada’s ability to sleuth culture and orchestrate 
this reunion resulted in a capital-M Moment for fashion lovers and cinephiles alike.

Creative Rule #5: Subvert Expectations.
Like a plot twist in a TV show, subverting expectations in brand marketing surprises 
and engages customers, drawing them deeper in the brand narrative or shifting their 
perception of the brand altogether. Crocs refused to market itself as a utilitarian 
boating shoe by leveraging celebrity association and high-fashion collaborations, 
turning fashion naysayers into believers. E-tailer SSENSE rewrote the retail social 
playbook by transforming its Instagram into an entertaining meme haven. Subvert -
ing expectations involves observing competitor behavior and deliberating behaving 
differently to stand out and stoke cultural intrigue.
Case Study: Pimple Patch About Town
Starface World, renowned for their ubiquitous star-shaped pimple patches, is no 
stranger to subversion. In its brand storytelling, product efficacy takes a backseat 
to fantasy-building: Starface engages its Gen Z audience with whimsical narratives 
featuring their packaging-turned-mascot, Big Yellow, embarking on adventures like 
riding the New York subway or training for a marathon. Across all digital touchpoints, 
the brand deploys story-driven maximalist aesthetics, rejecting the flat pastel min -
imalism we’re used to seeing with DTC skincare brands. Positioning itself more as a 
fashion statement than a mere acne solution, the brand has collaborated with Col -
lina Strada, Glossier, and Hello Kitty, earning its stripes as a symbol of individuality. 
Having a zit has truly never been cooler.

Creative Rule #6: Universe-Building.
Picture the brand universe that your devoted customer lives in: what are the sights, 
sounds, and textures? Is there silence or a Spotify playlist humming in the 
background? From runway to retail, cult brands create worlds drawn from the brand 
lifestyle for customers to escape to and imagine themselves in. The FRAMA Studio 
Store & Cafe in Copenhagen, embodies a cohesive lifestyle aesthetic in every detail, 
from the charming, hand-scribbled menu to the meticulously curated tableware and 
furniture. Bode’s LA flagship evokes the ambiance of an old-fashioned natural 
history museum: adorned with painted wooden signs, Mastodon ribs, and a plaster 
cast of a Dodo bird skeleton. Universe-building imbues products with context, 
turning them into souvenirs (and eventually icons) of a desired way of living. 
Case Study:  Artisanal Extravaganza!
John Galliano’s Maison Margiela Spring/Summer 2024 Artisanal show was a tes-
tament to his genius, transforming the underbelly of Pont Alexandre III in Paris into 
a fashion fantasy. Galliano immersed his audience in the  allure and enigma of the 
city’s streets with a set design reminiscent of a 1920s dive bar. Models moved as 
fully-realized characters rather than robotic clothes hangers. Pat McGrath’s glossy 
doll-like makeup added another dimension of awe, igniting countless TikTok tutori-
als. This theatrical showcase was a masterclass in universe-building, writing fashion 
history with its layers of storytelling and craftsmanship.

Creative Rule #7: Create A Countertrend.
Cult brands defy norms and create countertrends, not for the sake of being contrari -
an, but to make a statement about their beliefs and the world their devoted custom -
ers live in. In 1995, the groundbreaking Calvin Klein Collection boutique opened at 
654 Madison Avenue, underscoring Klein’s minimalist ethos. The space’s thought-
fully distilled design was a radical departure from convention, setting the tone for 
the next 25 years of retail design.4 Swimming against the currents of mainstream 
culture with your brand’s point of view creates differentiation and respect.
Case Study:  For Rich Eyes Only
The Row, the epitome of quiet luxury, made a bold move by banning social media 
at their fashion shows. By bucking the trend of instant, ubiquitous sharing, The Row 
not only encourages a more personal and genuine connection with their audience, 
but it also reinforces its core values of discretion and discernment. In line with 
its founders’ private lifestyles, the deliberate withdrawal from public consumption 
defines a new level of cult status for the brand, reminiscent of a time when fashion 
shows were about building lore and myth, free from the distractions of instant 
broadcast. Although this begs the question: if a model falls in the middle of the 
runway but no one is allowed to digitally document it… did it really happen?

Creative Rule #8: Creative Cross-Pollination.
Cult brands exist to fuel culture with inspiration. To do so, requires an understanding 
that innovation can come from anywhere, especially other industries. Apple’s for -
mer Senior Vice President Angela Ahrendts gave Apple stores its sleek luxury feel by 
drawing from her previous experience at Burberry. JACQUEMUS ignited the surrealist 
marketing craze by partnering with CGI artist Ian Pagdam to render oversized ver -
sions of its Bambino bag rolling down the streets of Paris. Creative cross-pollination 
doesn’t just cut through the noise, it creates its own. 
Case Study:  Prosthetic Performance Art
Makeup artist and drag queen Alexis Stone continues to break the Internet with 
his uncanny transformations, making surprise appearances at fashion weeks 
disguised as iconic figures such as Jennifer Coolidge, Lana Del Rey, Dolly Parton, 
Mrs. Doubtfire, and Glenn Close as Cruella de Vil. Each transformation is a 
months-long project, requiring the creation of prosthetic pieces, intricate hair and 
wig styling, extensive makeup testing, fake teeth, and collaboration with numerous 
artists to perfect each look. Stone’s ability to blend movie-quality transformations 
within a live cultural moment elicits curiosity, confusion, respect, and fascination 
all at once.
